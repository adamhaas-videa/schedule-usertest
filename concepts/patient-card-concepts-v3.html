What are they here for?

Every concept in v1 answered what happened last time. None of them answered the question a clinician actually walks up to the screen with: what is this appointment, and what has to get done in this chair before the patient stands up. v2 puts today on the card and demotes history to the one fact that changes today's work. Three concepts, each rendered for both tracks, at the card's real footprint.

What v1 got wrong The six needs were all retrospective — last visit, deferred treatment, the patient's quote. Useful in the huddle, but a card on an operatory timeline is read mid-day, between chairs. At that moment the appointment type is the field being scanned for, and it was nowhere on the card. The dimensions are unchanged: 280 × 228 in the list, 88 / 133 / 178px by duration in the grid.
The brief, restated

Five things today needs

These replace v1's six as the card's job. v1's six do not disappear — five of them collapse into T5, and the rest move to the drawer where a huddle can read them properly. Each specimen carries a coverage row: filled = on the card, outlined = present but truncated, dashed = pushed to the drawer.

T1What it isAppointment type, site — tooth, quadrant, arch — and booked duration.
T2What we're doingThe planned procedures in sequence, and how far through them we are.
T3What clears firstPrerequisites that gate the start: pre-med, lab case, INR, anesthesia plan.
T4What can't leave the roomBook the recall, re-present the deferred item, schedule the next step.
T5What changes today's workThe one carried-over fact that alters the plan. Everything else is drawer.
How the split is decided There is no separate visit-type field, and there should not be one. The track is a function of the procedure string — the app already does this in isHygieneProcedure(), matching prophylaxis, periodontal maintenance, scaling, root planing, srp, fluoride, sealants, deep cleaning, periodontal assessment. Everything else is the doctor track. One component, one derived flag, two content configurations — the markup never branches.
Concept D

Banner & Worklist

The appointment type is lifted out of the card body entirely and becomes a full-bleed strip across the top, tinted by track. Down a column of eight operatories it is the only thing you read at speed. Underneath, the body is the visit as a worklist — done, doing, to do — with the one action that cannot leave the room pinned to the floor. This is the loudest answer to “what are they here for,” and the card that is most useful during the appointment.

Why the banner is tinted rather than ruled. A colored edge on the card competes with the provider-color system already running on the avatars. A tinted header band reads as a classification of the appointment rather than an alert about it, holds an icon and the duration without stealing a body line, and survives at 88px where a body row does not.

Concept E

The Appointment Is the Headline

The most direct answer, and the most argumentative: the procedure takes the name's slot and the patient demotes to the meta line. On a schedule you already know who is in Op 3 at 10:30 — the grid told you. What you do not know is what the next hour is. The body then splits into two labelled halves: what happens today, and what has to be true before they leave.

The objection, and the answer. Demoting the patient name reads as wrong until you watch the screen get used: the name is confirmed at the chair, from the chart, out loud. The card is read from across the room to answer “what is happening in Op 3.” The name still sets in semibold at 11.5px and still opens the drawer — it is demoted, not hidden. Privacy mode already masks it, which is a second argument that the schedule cannot depend on it.

Concept F

Chair Arc

The quietest of the three and the only one shaped by time, which is what the card is sitting on. A visit has three gates — what must clear before, what happens in the chair, what must be true at the exit — and the card is that spine. The gates never move, so the eye lands rather than reads, and the card's emphasis follows the appointment's phase without changing its layout.

Degradation

What survives at 88 and 133

A 30-minute appointment gets 88px in the operatory grid; 45 minutes gets 133. The rule for v2 is simple and it is the whole argument for the banner: T1 never degrades. The appointment type is the last thing to go, not the first. Rendered at true size; the full card returns on hover.

Conditional logic

What the track actually changes

Every slot exists on both tracks. What differs is the vocabulary that fills it, the unit the site is measured in, and which gate carries the weight. Nothing here is a layout branch.

Slot Doctor track Hygiene track
T1 · Type Crown seat, crown prep, MOD composite, endo, extraction, implant placement. Verb + material. Prophy, perio maintenance, SRP, sealants, perio assessment. Interval + scope.
T1 · Site Tooth number — #14, #19. Occasionally a surface: MOD, DO. Quadrant or arch — UR/UL, 4 quad, full mouth. Never a single tooth.
T2 · Plan Sequential and irreversible. Order matters; you cannot cement before try-in. Sequential with a hand-off — the doctor exam interrupts mid-appointment and blocks dismissal.
T3 · Clears first Lab case received, pre-med taken, anesthesia plan, consent. Medical clearance (INR, anticoagulant, pre-med), radiograph interval, last charting date.
T4 · Exit Schedule the next step in the sequence (seat, post-op) and re-present the deferred item while they're still in the chair. Book the recall. The single field the PMS is worst at and the visit's actual deliverable. Plus re-present accepted-but-unbooked treatment.
T5 · Carry-over A declined item that is now accepted, or a finding that changes today's prep. Perio stage change, bleeding risk, recession watch, home-care compliance.
Actions Voice note · Perio · Review. Post-visit: note + images. Perio first · Voice note · Review. Post-visit: note + images.
Never on card Full tx plan, ledger balance, claim history, chart notes, the patient's quote. Full probing depths, recession map, home-care notes, historical prophy dates.
Where I'd land

The banner is settled; the body is the argument

These are not three competing cards. One of the three moves is a fix every version should take, and the other two are a real choice about what the body is for. Splitting them that way is the decision worth making in the review.

Take regardless

D's banner

Appointment type and site, tinted by track, above everything, at every size. It costs 28px, it is the field being scanned for, and it is the only element that makes the doctor/hygiene split legible down a column of eight operatories. Adopt it into whichever body wins.

Build first

F's gates, under it

Before · in chair · exit holds fixed positions, so the eye lands rather than reads, and it is the only body whose emphasis tracks the appointment's phase without rearranging. Critically, it is the only one that gives the exit action a permanent slot — the one item on the card that expires when the patient stands up.

Keep for the list

E — headline + bands

The strongest small card and the right treatment for list view and the huddle, where rows are wider and the reader is planning rather than operating. Demoting the patient name is the one decision here that needs a real user in front of it before it ships.

The dependency, restated from v1. v1 closed by noting that the retrospective fields live in chart-note prose and need extraction. v2's fields are the opposite problem: T1 and T2 are structured and already exist — procedure, duration, operatory, tooth — but Patient in mockPatients.ts carries only a flat procedure string and a loose visitTags array. Today's plan, prerequisites and exit actions have no home in the model yet. Before any of these can be built past a specimen, that shape needs naming: site, plan[], clears[], exit[]. The layout is downstream of that, and it is the cheap half.
